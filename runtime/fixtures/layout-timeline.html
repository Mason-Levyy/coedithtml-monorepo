2017 — Two of the three eventual founders met while working on an internal reporting tool at a logistics company, where they spent most of a year discovering that every team had quietly built its own spreadsheet because the official dashboards answered the wrong questions. Neither of them thought of it as a company at the time.
2018 — Built the first prototype on weekends, mostly to settle an argument about whether the problem was the tooling or the way the company was organized. It was both, but the prototype was convincing enough that a former colleague offered to be the first design partner if it ever became a real product.
2019 — Founded in a shared kitchen space above a hardware store, with three people and a spreadsheet. The first six months were spent talking to fifty potential customers before writing a line of code, which turned out to be the best decision the founders made. The spreadsheet that came out of those conversations still shapes the product roadmap.
2020 — Shipped the first version to five pilot customers, three of whom are still customers today. The product did roughly a tenth of what it does now and was held together by a nightly script that a founder ran by hand. Two of the five pilots churned within a quarter, and the exit interviews became the basis for the following year's work.
2021 — Raised a seed round and hired the first engineer who was not a founder, followed shortly by the first support hire. Revenue was small enough that the round was a bet on the customer conversations rather than the numbers. Most of the money went into replacing the nightly script with something that could run unattended.
2022 — Crossed 100 paying customers and, more usefully, crossed the point where support requests stopped being about bugs and started being about how to do things. The team grew to eleven people and moved out of the kitchen space into an office with a door that locked.
2023 — Opened a second office to cover European working hours after support response times became the most common complaint in renewal conversations. Shipped the API, which had been the single most requested feature for two years running and which turned out to drive more expansion revenue than any interface work done in the same period.
2024 — Reached profitability without raising again, at a headcount of twenty-six. The decision not to raise a Series A was contentious internally and cost the company two senior people who wanted to move faster. In hindsight it bought the independence to say no to a large customer who wanted the roadmap rewritten around them.
2025 — Rebuilt the data layer over nine months, a project that was estimated at three and was very nearly abandoned twice. The result cut query latency by roughly eighty percent and made the row-level security model possible, which had been blocked on the old schema since the beginning.
2026 — Passed a thousand customers and opened the free tier that had been debated since 2021. Early numbers suggest the free tier converts at about a third the rate of the trial it replaced, on roughly six times the volume, which is the outcome the model predicted and nobody quite believed.
