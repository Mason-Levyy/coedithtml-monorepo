Notes on Sourdough

Sourdough starts with a starter: a jar of flour and water left on the counter until the wild yeast and bacteria already present in the flour take hold and establish themselves. There is nothing to buy and nothing to add. The whole process is a matter of giving a population of microbes a predictable environment and then waiting long enough for them to sort themselves into a stable arrangement.

Feeding it daily for a week is usually enough to get a reliable rise, though the timeline varies with the temperature of your kitchen. In a warm room the culture may be ready in four days. In a cold one it can take two weeks, and during that time it will pass through a phase where it smells strongly of acetone and appears to have died. It has not died. It is simply working through the succession of organisms that precede the ones you actually want.

Some bakers keep starters for decades and attach a great deal of significance to their age. In practice a starter reflects the flour it has been fed and the temperature it has been kept at far more than it reflects its own history. A starter given to you by a friend will, within a month of living in your kitchen and eating your flour, behave much like one you raised yourself.

The dough itself is mostly patience. Folding the dough over on itself every thirty minutes for the first few hours does more for gluten development than kneading ever did, and it requires almost no effort. You are not trying to work the dough so much as give the flour time to hydrate and the gluten time to organize on its own.

This is the part that most often gets rushed. The temptation is to treat the folds as a task to be completed rather than a schedule to be kept, and to do them all in twenty minutes because you need to leave the house. The dough will not cooperate. Nothing you do in twenty minutes substitutes for three hours of intermittent attention.

A long cold rise in the refrigerator overnight develops a depth of flavor that a same-day loaf never quite reaches. Twelve hours is good and eighteen is better. Beyond about twenty-four hours the acidity begins to degrade the gluten and the loaf loses structure, going slack and spreading out in the oven instead of rising.

The cold rise also makes the dough dramatically easier to handle. Cold dough is firm and holds a shape; room-temperature dough at the same hydration is slack and sticks to everything it touches. If you have struggled with shaping, the problem is very often temperature rather than technique.

Shaping is about building surface tension. You want the outer skin of the loaf stretched taut over the interior so it holds its form under its own weight and directs the expansion upward rather than outward. The specific folding sequence matters much less than whether the result feels tight when you press it.

Scoring the top right before baking controls where the loaf splits open in the oven. Without a score it will still split, but it will do so wherever the crust happens to be weakest, which is usually somewhere ugly along the side. A single confident slash at a shallow angle is more effective than an elaborate pattern cut too timidly.

A cast iron pot with a lid traps the steam coming off the loaf for the first twenty minutes of the bake. That steam keeps the crust soft and pliable while the interior is still expanding, which is what allows the dramatic rise people associate with good bread. Remove the lid too early and the crust sets before the loaf has finished growing.

After the lid comes off, the remaining twenty to twenty-five minutes are about color. A pale loaf is underbaked regardless of what the interior thermometer says; most of what people describe as the flavor of good bread is developed in the crust during this final stretch, and pulling it early to avoid over-browning is the most common mistake in home baking.

Let it cool completely before cutting. The interior is still setting as it cools, and cutting into a hot loaf releases the steam that is in the middle of being reabsorbed into the crumb. The result is gummy in a way that has nothing to do with the bake and everything to do with impatience. This is genuinely difficult and nobody manages it every time.
